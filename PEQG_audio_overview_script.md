Hi, I'm Yemko Pryor from the Lasisi Lab at the University of Michigan, with collaborators at UCLA and the University of Minnesota. My poster is *Testing Network-Based Predictions of Genetic Constraint in Melanogenesis*, and it asks a simple question about the genes that build pigment: are evolutionary constraint and recent adaptation acting on the same genes, or on different ones?

I work in the melanogenesis network — about 129 genes from Raghunath and colleagues. What makes this pathway a useful test case is that it spans a huge range of tissue specificity. At one end are broadly expressed master regulators, like MITF and KIT, switched on across nearly every tissue. At the other are pigment enzymes, like TYR and TYRP1, active almost only in skin. My first figure lays out that gradient.

My first question is about constraint — which genes can't tolerate being broken, measured by LOEUF. I find that pleiotropy predicts it, in two independent ways. Genes that are broadly expressed, and genes that sit in many pathways, are each more constrained, and they hold up independently of one another in a joint model. So the broadly expressed, highly connected genes face the strongest purifying selection, while the tissue-restricted pigment enzymes are far more tolerant of loss of function.

My second question is about adaptation. Using the population branch statistic, I look at where African and Melanesian lineages show the most population-specific differentiation across these genes. And here's the striking part: the two populations sit at opposite ends of the tissue-specificity axis. African differentiation is concentrated in broadly expressed genes; Melanesian differentiation, in tissue-restricted ones. European and South Asian lineages show no signal — a clean lineage-specificity control.

Now the key link. Loss-of-function constraint does not predict where this differentiation falls — the two sets of genes are statistically indistinguishable in LOEUF, with a p-value of about 0.97. So constraint and adaptation are tracking different things. Constraint tracks pleiotropy. Differentiation tracks tissue specificity.

That's the takeaway: pleiotropy constrains the *type* of variation that can fix, not *which* genes can diverge between populations. A broadly expressed gene can be intolerant of being knocked out and still adapt through subtler regulatory or missense changes that loss-of-function metrics never see.

I'm careful to call these patterns consistent with selection rather than proof of it, and the analysis sits within this 129-gene network. But together, the figures tell one story: in the genes that shape human pigmentation, constraint and adaptation are partly decoupled. Thanks for listening — I'd love to talk more at the poster.
